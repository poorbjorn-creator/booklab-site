You're Not Dead Yet

How grief and loss can teach you to live - if you let them.

by David Ferrugio
📣 Highlight

What's It About?

David Ferrugio lost his father on September 11th, 2001. He was twelve years old. He's spent the decades since exploring grief, death, and what comes after - through raw storytelling and hundreds of conversations on his DEAD Talks Podcast (over a million followers strong).

This isn't a self-help book with five tidy stages and a neat resolution. It's an honest companion for the messy, unpredictable journey of grief. Ferrugio doesn't pretend to have answers. Instead, he offers something rarer: understanding. He weaves his own experiences with wisdom gathered from real conversations about loss, near-death experiences, and what it actually means to live while carrying grief.

I started this as an audiobook - narrated by Ferrugio himself - and the narration is excellent. His voice carries the weight and warmth this subject needs. I liked it so much I got the physical copy too.

🎯 Why It's on BookLab

I've read about the trouble of death and our finite existence. In The Denial of Death, Top Five Regrets of the Dying, and even in fictional classics like Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich. That's about our own mortality and how to live with the terrible fact that we are going to die. Now it's time to look at grief and loss.

Most of us have lost someone we love. It wreaks havoc with our inner worlds.

Who did you lose? I lost my best friend. It was 20 years ago. Still f@$ing hurts.

You're Not Dead Yet by David Ferrugio in my ears, until the physical copy arrives.

📖 Key Topics

  • Permission to feel - giving yourself the green light to grieve, to laugh, to feel joy, all without apology
  • The rules we didn't write - societal expectations around grief that fog our minds and limit our healing
  • Choosing your response - what's in your control and what isn't, especially in moments of crisis
  • Raw storytelling - Ferrugio's personal losses (starting with 9/11) woven through with honesty and vulnerability
  • Near-death experiences & the afterlife - drawn from hundreds of podcast conversations
  • Spiritual exploration - not preachy, just open and searching
  • Living while grieving - grief as a teacher, not a prison

📝 Highlights from My Reading

Here are some moments that stuck with me - pulled straight from pages I highlighted.

The Power of Permission

"You're allowed to not be okay - and still want happiness."
- Michael Allio

This chapter title alone is worth the price of admission. Permission. Such a simple word, but when you're deep in it - grief, loss, uncertainty - it's the one thing you forget to give yourself.

I'm struggling at the moment. The company I work for will go bankrupt any day now, unless there's a deus ex machina. I'm on my second month without a paycheck. When shit happens, it feels like you're expected to feel and act by a set of rules. It could be the death of a loved one, divorce, job loss, bankruptcy - you name it. People around me who know act differently toward me. I act differently too.

It's tough, but I'm not that fazed by things I can't control (like my employer's finances). I'm actually quite cheerful at times, and sometimes that rubs people the wrong way. But you don't need anyone's permission to find joy again, even if that joy is fleeting.

Finding joy when the rules say you shouldn't

"I've learned to give myself permission to feel and permission to find joy again - even in moments when my paradigms and current state of emotions tell me I shouldn't. Because it is okay to feel and it is okay to find joy."

"I can't believe they're dating already; it's only been 137 years since their spouse died." Ferrugio nails it. The societal rules we see in movies, gossip, and stigmatized values about how we should grieve or feel - they fog our minds. There are no rules here. You don't need anyone's permission to find joy again.

Echoes of Stoicism

I hear echoes of Stoicism as I read through this book. The Dichotomy of Control is a foundational Stoic principle: you must separate life into things you can control and things you cannot control to find inner peace. Easier said than done - that's why we practice Stoicism during good times, not only the bad.

One of my key takeaways from Seneca's Letters from a Stoic is this: preparing for adversity in good times is one of the best ways to keep a cool head when adversity knocks on your door and it's most needed - for both yourself and those who depend on you.

I don't know David Ferrugio's relationship with the Stoics, but in a dramatic passage about his mom having a cardiac arrest, his words hint of Stoic teachings. He writes about knowing what was in his control and what wasn't - and choosing his response, even when every instinct screamed fight or flight.

🎯 Who Is This Book For?

If you're acutely dealing with loss and grief right now - this book will feel like a conversation with someone who gets it. No clinical detachment, no five-step programs. Just a very human discussion about the struggles we face in life and the possibility of dealing with even the hardest moments.

It's also for you if you've experienced loss and hurt but feel like you never worked through it properly. The kind that sits in the background, quietly shaping how you move through life. Ferrugio doesn't rush you. He just opens the door.

This is not an academic take on the process of grief. It's raw, it's real, and it meets you where you are.
