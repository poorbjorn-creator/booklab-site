October 2026

The most interesting nonfiction books coming out this month

October 2026 · 11 Books

The death of reading versus the case for ditching your phone. The AGI race from inside the labs and from the desk of someone working alongside it. A memoir that says attention beats willpower, released the same day as the tenth anniversary of the book that made willpower cool again. And the Gold Rush that cracked the republic. Eleven nonfiction picks, zero filler.

01
The New Dark Ages: The Death of Reading and the Dawn of the Post-Literate Society
James Marriott
Culture / Media Theory / Human Nature October 6

A Times (London) columnist argues that the collapse of reading is the most important cultural shift of our time. Rising literacy built the modern world: revolutions, arguments about individual rights and the purpose of government, scientific truth. Now the number of people who read is in free fall, university students can't get through their assigned books, and the chain of knowledge linking reader to reader is breaking for the first time since the fall of the Western Roman Empire. A defense of the written word and long-form argument against the culture of the screen. Already a Sunday Times bestseller in the UK.

Bjorn's take: "This one is personal. I've spent 11 years telling people to read books, and the trend line is going the wrong direction. The Shallows made the case that the internet is rewiring our brains. Marriott is saying the damage is done and we're living in the aftermath. A book about the death of reading, published for readers, reviewed on a channel about why books matter. That's either the most BookLab thing imaginable or the most depressing. Probably both."
Read if:
  • You've noticed your own attention span shrinking and want to understand why
  • You care about what we lose when a culture stops reading
  • You want a short, urgent book that makes the case for the thing you already do

02
You Don't Need a Smartphone
August Lamm
Attention Economy / Digital Minimalism October 6

August Lamm wasted her youth on the internet. In her late twenties, burnt out as a social media influencer, she sold her smartphone and switched to a dumb phone. Her mental health improved, her attention span came back, her curiosity returned. Now she's written a manifesto to help you do the same. Short, cheap, and unapologetically prescriptive - the practical counterpart to Marriott's diagnosis at #1.

Bjorn's take: "Deep Work made the intellectual case for protecting your attention. This makes the personal case, someone who actually did it and documented what happened. I appreciate people doing experiments, and in this case I want to hear the results. I use my phone constantly for BookLab (and everything else, basically), and I'm not sure the tradeoff is always worth it. Pairs with Marriott at #1: he says reading is dying, she says here's one reason why, and here's the fix. Diagnosis and prescription, same month."
Read if:
  • You've thought about downgrading your phone but haven't pulled the trigger
  • You want a short, practical book, not another 400-page treatise
  • You read Marriott at #1 and want the prescription after the diagnosis

03
The AGI Chronicles: The Inside Story of the Race to Create an Artificial Superintelligence
Kevin Roose
Technology / AI / Existential Risk October 6

The story of the twenty-first century's Manhattan Project. Drawing on more than 150 interviews with insiders at OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind, plus unpublished company documents and research memos, Roose reconstructs the decade-long sprint to build artificial general intelligence. New detail on the disagreements that led Anthropic's founders to leave OpenAI, fresh reporting on Sam Altman's firing and reinstatement, Google's scramble to catch up, and the White House officials trying to regulate a technology improving faster than they can understand it. Blurbed by Walter Isaacson and Ray Kurzweil. 448 pages.

Bjorn's take: "I reviewed Futureproof (but it was almost a decade since I read it) and Roose is a solid tech writer, clear-eyed, not a cheerleader. I work with AI every day and I'm deeply curious about what's actually happening inside these labs. The Isaacson blurb tells me this might be one of the top AI books of the year."
Read if:
  • You want the inside story of the AGI race from someone who was in the room
  • You want the drama behind the headlines: the defections, the firing, the rivalries
  • You followed September's Socher-vs-Turkle AI debate and want the behind-the-curtain sequel

04
Co-Existence: The Next Phase of AI
Ethan Mollick
AI / Work / Human Potential October 20

How do you live alongside machines that are sometimes smarter than you? Wharton professor Ethan Mollick - author of the bestseller Co-Intelligence and the One Useful Thing newsletter - argues that the future of AI will be defined by interaction, not replacement. Drawing on years of research and hands-on experimentation, he shows how to work productively with AI while keeping your own judgment, where human insight still matters most, and how to avoid becoming dependent on systems built to persuade and imitate. Blurbed by Angela Duckworth and Tyler Cowen.

Roose tells you how we got here. Mollick tells you what to do on Monday morning.

Bjorn's take: "I just read Human Again in the Age of AI, which revolves around this topic. Yet I feel that there is more to learn. I just fired up my own agents on Grok Bot this week and started experimenting with delegating all types of tasks. Agents are a different beast than using ChatGPT as a glorified Google search machine. You really need to play around with these tools to understand where we are heading. Two AI books in one month sounds like a lot, but they cover completely different ground: Roose has the story, Mollick has the playbook."
Read if:
  • You use AI every day and want to use it without outsourcing your thinking
  • You read Co-Intelligence and want to know what's changed since
  • You want the practical AI book of the month, not the dramatic one

05
Why We Ask Why: The Science of Explanation and the Human Drive to Understand
Tania Lombrozo
Cognitive Science / Philosophy / Human Nature October 6

Humans are explanation-seeking creatures. We wonder why there's traffic, why a friend behaved a certain way, why the sky is blue, and - in our more existential moments - why we're here at all. Princeton psychologist Tania Lombrozo has spent twenty-five years turning that instinct back on itself. What does explanation actually do for us? Why do some explanations feel more satisfying than others? Why do we prefer simple ones, and should we? A book about the cognitive habit underneath science, learning, and making sense of your own life. 288 pages.

Bjorn's take: "This is the kind of book that doesn't get attention on release day but ends up being the one you keep thinking about. I've read a lot about how we think - Thinking, Fast and Slow, The Righteous Mind, Maps of Meaning - but this goes a layer deeper, and brings my thoughts to David Deutsch's phenomenal book The Beginning of Infinity. That book rocked my world and gave me a deeper understanding of humans as explanation creators. I hope this book gives even more depth to the topic."
Read if:
  • You liked Thinking, Fast and Slow and want the next layer down
  • You want to know why bad explanations can feel so convincing
  • You're building a philosophy-of-mind shelf, not a productivity shelf

06
Vibe: The Secrets of Strong Connections in a Lonely World
Adam Grant
Psychology / Connection / Human Nature October 13

When we vibe with other people, it fuels our happiness and our success. Yet building meaningful ties has become one of the most pressing problems of our time, as face-to-face interaction moves behind screens, communication gets outsourced to AI, and the world grows more polarized. Grant digs into the surprising science of chemistry and closeness to show how to forge faster, stronger, longer-lasting bonds.

Bjorn's take: "I reviewed Think Again and Grant is not a reliable writer. Think Again was a mediocre book. But his books are clear, well-researched, and not afraid to challenge his own audience. The loneliness crisis connects several interests I have when it comes to human connection, attachment and peak living, covered in books such as Lost Connections, Attached, even Flow. What I'm curious about is whether Grant has something genuinely new to say or whether this is a well-packaged summary of what we already know. Either way, the topic matters."
Read if:
  • You've felt the pull of screens replacing real conversation
  • You want the science behind why some people click and others don't
  • You're interested in the loneliness epidemic beyond the headlines

07
Will and Attention: A Memoir
Meghan O'Gieblyn
Memoir / Philosophy / Self-Mastery October 6

After leaving evangelical Christianity, kicking an addiction, and building a life as a writer, Meghan O'Gieblyn was admired by her friends for having a strong will. Then, in her late thirties, she started secretly attending Catholic mass - and, after eleven years of sobriety, began drinking again. Knowledge and determination had failed her. So she turned to a line from the philosopher Simone Weil: "We have to try to cure our faults by attention and not by will." From the author of God, Human, Animal, Machine. Starred Kirkus review. 288 pages.

Read it next to the Daily Stoic at #8. Same release day, opposite answers to the same question: how do you actually change?

Bjorn's take: "This is the book I didn't see coming. Most of the self-mastery shelf - and I've read a lot of it - assumes willpower is the answer. O'Gieblyn lived that assumption, and it broke down on her. Simone Weil's idea that attention, not will, is the real engine of change is one of those lines that rearranges how you think. Pairs perfectly with the Daily Stoic at #8: Holiday says discipline is destiny, O'Gieblyn says discipline almost destroyed her. Same release day, opposite answers. That's October in a nutshell. I also like the riches-to-rags element. Gets me to think about a hidden gem I read a while back: the memoir Drinking: A Love Story."
Read if:
  • You read God, Human, Animal, Machine and want more of that mind
  • You're quietly suspicious of willpower culture
  • You want the philosophical memoir of the month, not the celebrity one

08
The Daily Stoic: 10th Anniversary Edition
Ryan Holiday & Stephen Hanselman
Philosophy / Stoicism / Self-Mastery October 6

Ten years and more than three million copies on, the book that launched the modern Stoic revival returns updated and expanded: a new introduction, more than sixty new translations, two bonus chapters, and new illustrations. 366 daily meditations drawing on Marcus Aurelius, Seneca, Epictetus, and lesser-known Stoics like Zeno and Musonius Rufus. 464 pages.

Bjorn's take: "I've reviewed more Ryan Holiday books than any other author: Ego Is the Enemy, Stillness Is the Key, Discipline Is Destiny, Courage Is Calling, Right Thing, Right Now. The Daily Stoic was my entry point to his work and to practical Stoicism in general. Ten years later, Stoicism is everywhere, and this book is a big reason why. If you haven't read it, start here. If you have, the anniversary edition is a good excuse to revisit."
Read if:
  • You want a daily Stoic practice without reading Marcus Aurelius in the original
  • You've read the original and want to see what a decade of reflection adds
  • You read O'Gieblyn at #7 and want the case for the will

Bjorn's Pick of the Month 09
Sleeping Beauties: Why Good Ideas Go Dormant and How They Wake Up
David Byrne
Creativity / History of Ideas / Human Potential October 6

It's nice to believe in a meritocracy of ideas, where genius is always recognized and innovation always rewarded. In reality, many of the world's great creators got little traction at first, and their work only came roaring back later. Dark matter, lichen, and continental drift. Solar panels and the steam engine. Bach, Bruegel, Vermeer, and Melville. All were sleeping beauties. Across 35 short histories, the Talking Heads founder asks why good ideas fall asleep and what wakes them up.

Bjorn's take: "Great ideas exist, they just get buried by bad timing or bad luck. The question is whether Byrne finds a bigger pattern across all those sleeping beauties, or whether it stays a collection of interesting anecdotes. Either way, I'm curious. Since I'm studying the history of scientific revolutions this year, I think this one is worth keeping an eye out for."
Read if:
  • You're fascinated by why some ideas succeed and others vanish
  • You want a creative polymath's take on the history of breakthroughs
  • You liked How Music Works and want Byrne thinking about everything else

10
Blockers: Rebels in the Deep State
Michael Lewis
Politics / Narrative Nonfiction / Institutions October 6

In the new Washington, being a "Blocker" was the worst thing you could be: a holdover, an impediment, someone who didn't belong. Michael Lewis found a handful of federal employees targeted by DOGE - people who believed they had a calling, and then discovered they were no longer wanted. One kept Americans' most sensitive tax data out of the wrong hands. Another made sure politicians and civil servants played by the rules. One figured out how to stop wildfires from destroying suburbs; another helped uncover the mysteries of cystic fibrosis. There was also a security guard. The question Lewis leaves you with: if these people can't survive in government, what does that say about us? 256 pages.

Bjorn's take: "Michael Lewis is supposed to be one of the best narrative nonfiction writers alive, and I'm yet to read a single book from him. The Fifth Risk already made the case that the boring parts of government are the most important parts, and that was before DOGE showed up with a sledgehammer. Lewis doesn't do ideology. He finds the human story inside the system and makes you care about people you didn't know existed. Pairs with Philbrick at #11 - institutions under strain, 2025 and 1848. Same question, different century. I'm not really a big government cheerleader, but I'm curious to hear this perspective."
Read if:
  • You want the DOGE story told by a storyteller, not a pundit
  • You liked The Big Short, The Fifth Risk, or Going Infinite
  • You're interested in what happens when institutions face a wrecking ball
Buy on Amazon
11
The Rush: California Gold, the Civil War, and the Making of the Modern World
Nathaniel Philbrick
History / Power & Ambition October 6

In January 1848, a carpenter spotted flecks of gold in a stream at Sutter's Mill, triggering the greatest voluntary migration in U.S. history. Philbrick turns the Gold Rush from a tale of sudden riches into the origin story of America's modern contradictions: capital consolidated at dizzying speed, democracy in thrall to private power, xenophobia weaponized in the name of liberty. Following the gold from riverbed to countinghouse to the halls of power, he shows how vigilantism hardened into law and how the fight over slavery in the West helped push the nation toward civil war. From the author of In the Heart of the Sea and Mayflower. 464 pages.

Pairs with Lewis at #10: institutions under strain, 1848 and now.

Bjorn's take: "I'm a sucker for well-told history, and Philbrick is one of the best in the business. The Gold Rush is one of those events everyone knows about but nobody really understands. The greed, the violence, the way it reshaped an entire nation in a few years. Capital consolidating at speed, democracy bending to private power. Sound familiar? That's why it pairs with Lewis at #10. One is 1848, the other is now. Same structural question."
Read if:
  • You want history that reads like an adventure novel
  • You liked In the Heart of the Sea, Empire of the Summer Moon, or Killers of the Flower Moon
  • You're interested in the moments that made (or broke) America
