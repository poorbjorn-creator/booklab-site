The Black Swan

We can't predict the future — and the experts are even worse at it than you are.

by Nassim Nicholas Taleb

Our Blindness to Randomness

This book is about our blindness to randomness. Especially when it comes to large deviations and what Nassim calls a "black swan" event.

A "black swan" is a highly improbable event with three principal characteristics: it's unpredictable, it carries a massive impact, and after the fact, we concoct an explanation that makes it look less random and more predictable than it actually was.

Examples of black swans? 9/11. The amazing success of Google.

Why are we so bad at acknowledging these types of events? We concentrate on what we know and fail to take into consideration what we don't know. We love to oversimplify, narrate, and categorize a complex reality. This hurts us when estimating risks and seizing opportunities.

"I make a claim against many of our habits of thought, that our world is dominated by the extreme, the unknown and the very improbable."

The Triplet of Opacity

The human mind suffers from three ailments as it comes into contact with history, what Taleb calls the Triplet of Opacity:

🔮 The illusion of understanding — everyone thinks they know what's going on, when the world is actually very complicated and random.
🔙 Retrospective distortion — how we can only assess things in hindsight, making the past look predictable.
📊 Overvaluation of facts — the handicap of authoritative and learned people who mistake information for knowledge.

Notes & Highlights

Anti-KnowledgeOnly a few people understand that unread books are more valuable than read ones. Focus on anti-knowledge — what you don't know.
The Movie Makes the ActorOften someone's success is more luck than actual skill. The excellence of a director or actor is assigned after the fact.
The Narrative FallacyCompare: "the king died and the queen died" with "the king died and then the queen died of grief." The narrative of the second sentence reduces two pieces of information into one, even though it's adding information. Every recall is a reframe — every time you bring a memory back, it can be changed a bit to fit the current narrative.
More Information ≠ Better PredictionsHaving more information won't make your predictions any better, but it will make you more certain they are. Listening to the news on the radio every hour is far worse for you than reading a weekly magazine — the longer intervals allow information to be filtered.
"In order to predict the future you need to know about technologies that will be discovered in the future. But that knowledge would almost automatically make us able to start developing those technologies right away. Ergo, we don't know what we will know."
Silent EvidenceThose who didn't make it usually don't tell their story. The cemetery of failed attempts is smaller among aspiring store clerks than aspiring writers.
The Hedonic EffectSeveral evenly distributed pieces of good news are better than one piece of great news. Rewards don't have to be large — but plenty!
The Barbell StrategyMaximize your exposure to positive black swans by investing small amounts in many risky companies — and the rest in extremely risk-free, boring investments. When you buy the risky stock, you're aware of the risk. Black swans are never taken into account for mid-risk companies, creating false security.

Like Taleb, I refuse to run to catch trains. Missing a train is only painful if you run after it.

⭐️ Takeaway

We can't predict future events for shit. But more importantly, people that work with predictions are even worse predictors than your average Joe.

⚖️ Verdict

Great book! Taleb makes a powerful case that we're all walking around with blinders on, building models of reality that ignore the most important events — the ones we can't see coming. It's a book that changes how you think about risk, success, and how much you really know. If you liked this, his follow-up Antifragile takes the ideas further — from "we can't predict" to "here's how to benefit from chaos."
