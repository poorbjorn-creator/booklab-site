Grinding It Out

A 52-year-old milkshake machine salesman walks into a hamburger stand in San Bernardino — and builds the most recognizable restaurant on Earth.

by Ray Kroc

The Man Behind the Golden Arches

Ray Kroc was 52 years old when he first met the McDonald brothers in San Bernardino, California. He was a multimixer salesman — not exactly the résumé you'd expect from someone who'd go on to build one of the most successful companies in history. But he was impressed with the operation they had going: the limited menu, the affordable prices, the cleanliness. And those French fries. It was love. ❤️

Ray wondered why the brothers didn't expand their concept, but the McDonald brothers were not men of such ambition. So Ray took on the expansion himself — and the rest is history.

From the Part to the Whole

What struck me most about Ray's approach was that he didn't see his ideas as some grand design. He worked from the part to the whole. He didn't move to the large-scale ideas before he had perfected the details. Perfect every fundamental of your business if you want it to perform well.

I wonder if this mindset could be helpful for those of us building Instagram accounts or YouTube channels? Would it be beneficial to focus on perfecting one element at a time — pictures, captions, hashtags, timings — before worrying about the big picture?

"Persistence and determination alone is omnipotent."

The Monotony Index

Here's a detail I loved: they started to build restaurants based on what they called a "monotony index." The higher the degree of monotony in a town, the higher the likelihood that a McDonald's restaurant would succeed. Why? Less alternatives for spending the evening. Brilliant in its simplicity.

A People Business

Ray joked about being able to engineer a row of machines that prepared the meal for the customers, but said they never would. McDonald's is a people business. "The smile on that counter girl's face as she takes your order is a vital part of our image."

He also had sharp things to say about ambition and risk:

"Young Americans these days don't get the chance to enjoy work. Much of this country's social and political philosophy seems aimed at removing the risk from life, one by one."
"It is no achievement to walk a tightrope laid flat on the floor. Where there is no risk there can be no pride in achievement and consequently no happiness."

He didn't see his dreams as wasted energy either, as they were always linked to some form of action. "When I dreamed about having a lemonade stand, it wasn't long until I had a lemonade stand."

The Rough Edges

I should mention: the book has a somewhat outdated view of women, and Ray brags quite a bit toward the end. But surprisingly little drama overall. I feel the rise of McDonald's was a pretty smooth ride compared to other business stories I've read.

🍟 My best combo at the Golden Arches these days is a Spring Salad and a McVegan burger. Wicked combo! 👌

📖 Why Read Memoirs and Biographies?

There's something uniquely powerful about reading someone's life story. You get decades of experience — the failures, the breakthroughs, the moments of doubt — compressed into a few hundred pages. Memoirs like this one let you borrow someone else's perspective and test it against your own life. It's the closest thing to living multiple lives.

💡 Key Takeaway

Work from the part to the whole. Perfect every fundamental before moving on to the large-scale idea. Ray didn't dream in abstractions — he dreamed in action. When he imagined a lemonade stand, he built one. That's the difference between a dreamer and a doer.

⚖️ Verdict

I'm thankful for this little memoir of the first 40 years of McDonald's. Love it or hate it, McDonald's is a company we all have some kind of relationship with. It's not the most gripping business story — surprisingly smooth compared to others — but Ray's voice is genuine and his hustle is undeniable. A solid read if you're into business memoirs.
