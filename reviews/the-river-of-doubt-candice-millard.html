The River of Doubt

A former president, an uncharted river, and the Amazon at its most brutal. This book reads like fiction — but every word is true.

by Candice Millard

The Bull Moose Gets Shot

In 1912 Theodore Roosevelt was shot in the chest by some crazy saloonkeeper on his way to deliver a speech on his third-term election campaign. The bullet was slowed by his spectacle case and speech manuscript in his inner pocket, which kept it from reaching his lungs or heart. With blood dripping from his shirt and the bullet hole clearly visible through his notes, he spoke the now famous line: "It takes more than that to kill a Bull Moose!" — before delivering a 90-minute speech in front of an awestruck crowd.

Not long after that, he suffered a great defeat in the presidential election. Defeat was never easy for Theodore.

Into the Unknown

He took to physical exhaustion and the wilderness to think sharp, fight depression and recover from loss. Roosevelt was not getting younger and wanted a last big adventure. Together with Brazilian explorer Rondon, he set out on an extraordinary journey to map an uncharted river in the most inhospitable parts of the Amazon rainforest.

It was going to be a rough ride. Even for a "Rough Rider."

🗺️ The Expedition at a Glance

📅 When: 1913–1914
📍 Where: Rio da Dúvida ("River of Doubt"), Brazilian Amazon
🚣 Distance: ~1,000 miles of uncharted river
⚠️ Outcome: Roosevelt nearly died — lost 55 pounds and never fully recovered

The Amazon Doesn't Care Who You Are

This book reads like fiction and I'm once again amazed by Theodore Roosevelt's character.

I'm also amazed by the Amazon, but I don't want to set my foot there myself. Hearing about penis-entering fish, malaria, the skull-crushing fall of Brazil nuts from their trees and cannibalistic tribes (maybe not so much anymore…) make me suffice with reading about it.

🦟 Malaria — multiple members struck down, including Roosevelt himself
🌊 Rapids — canoes smashed, supplies lost, men drowned
🐟 Piranhas & candirú — yes, that fish. The Amazon doesn't mess around
🔪 Murder — one expedition member killed another over a stolen can of food
🌳 Starvation — supplies ran out, the jungle offered little to eat
🏹 Indigenous arrows — unseen tribes watching from the jungle, occasionally attacking

Marcus Aurelius in the Jungle

On a side note: Roosevelt brought Meditations by Marcus Aurelius on the expedition. He said he took great comfort in reading it during the worst parts of the journey.

Great packing advice for dangerous adventures. 😀

💪 "I'll Make My Body"

When Roosevelt was a boy, his father sat down with him and said: "You have the power to change your fate. You have a great mind but you don't have the body. Without the body, the mind can't go as far as it should. You must make your body. It's hard work, but I know you will do it."

Roosevelt's answer: "I'll make my body!"

He did. And then he spent the rest of his life proving it — from the Dakota Badlands to the presidency to this very expedition.

💡 Key Takeaway

Some people respond to defeat by retreating. Roosevelt responded by heading straight into the most dangerous jungle on Earth. The River of Doubt is a reminder that the impulse to push yourself — physically, mentally, to the absolute edge — can be both your greatest strength and the thing that nearly destroys you.

⚖️ Verdict

This book reads like the best adventure novel you've ever picked up — except it all happened. Candice Millard's writing is vivid, tense, and perfectly paced. You feel the humidity, the desperation, and the sheer willpower it took to survive. If you've ever been curious about Theodore Roosevelt — or if you just want a jaw-dropping story about human endurance — this is your book. One of the best nonfiction books I've read.
